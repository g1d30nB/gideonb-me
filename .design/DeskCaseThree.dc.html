The challenge

Twenty five years of directing the work. Somewhere in there the answer to what does good look like when the system is generative and probabilistic stopped being something I could give honestly from the sidelines.

So I built one. PrepCall is a live AI voice coach for interview practice. You talk to it for twenty minutes and it returns a coaching report grounded in what you actually said and how you said it.

My role

Solo founder. Product strategy, the voice interaction model, every flow, the report architecture, the build on Hume and the Anthropic API, the deployment, and the compliance work.

What I learned building it

The failure that taught me the most.

Somewhere around the fourth session per user, quality started degrading, and it took a while to see why. Nothing was broken. The system prompt had grown from roughly 5,600 characters at session one to over 34,000 by session four. Context had simply accumulated until the model was drowning in its own history.

The fix was architectural, not cosmetic.

A bounded prompt model with hard character budgets, enforced regardless of how many sessions a user has had. That is what designing for a probabilistic system actually involves. The interface is the easy part. The hard part is designing for a product that behaves differently on Tuesday than it did on Monday, and building the constraints that keep it trustworthy anyway.

Regulation as a design input.

PrepCall coaches the candidate rather than assessing them for an employer, because Article 5 of the EU AI Act prohibits emotion inference in the workplace. The regulation did not limit the design. It chose it, and it chose better. I owned the compliance layer personally, including the classification and the data protection impact assessment.

Signal is not meaning.

Voice models can read hesitation, nervousness and confidence. None of that is useful until something decides what it means in context. That gap is the design problem, and it is the thesis behind Emotrix, the middleware I am building next.

Where it stands

PrepCall is live and in market with real users. There is no revenue and there are no paying customers: it is craft evidence rather than a business, and I would rather say so than dress it up.

What it bought me is a point of view I could not have borrowed. Seven years of leadership and a year of building, and most of what I now think about AI in product comes from shipping rather than from reading.

The pattern

The pattern across all three is the same. Find the thing that is actually broken, which is usually structural rather than visual. Build the system that stops it recurring. Measure it in the language the business already uses. Then hand it to the people closest to the work and hold the bar.

The last part is the one people underestimate.

Most of what I am proudest of was drawn by somebody else.
